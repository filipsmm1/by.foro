The Arched Range Hood, Drawn Properly

Begin with the insert, duct and working clearance. The curve belongs around the appliance, never the other way round.

by.foro Editorial26 August 202611 min read

The kitchen plan

  2. Why the arch works
  3. Ventilation first
  4. Get the proportion right
  5. Choose the finish
  6. Fit it to the room
  7. Mistakes to avoid

An arched range hood is a curved enclosure built around a functioning kitchen exhaust system. The most timeless version matches the width of the range, begins its curve above the working clearance and uses one quiet finish such as plaster, limewash, wood or stone. Design the ventilation insert and duct route first, then draw the arch around them.

The order matters. An arched hood is often treated as a decorative backdrop because photographs flatten the mechanical parts. In the room, it must capture cooking vapour, sit at the correct height, allow filters to be removed and meet the clearances specified for the range and hood insert. A beautiful curve that makes the fan hard to use is simply expensive scenery.

The search interest is real. Houzz reported in May 2026 that US searches for “arched range hood” rose 177 per cent year over year, part of a wider move toward scalloped tile, curved islands and softer architectural geometry. The useful version of the trend is not a kitchen filled with arches. It is one curve placed where the room already needs a visual centre.

The concise answer

Build the arch around the appliance, not the reverse

Choose the range and ventilation insert first. Confirm hood width, mounting height, duct diameter, airflow, filter access, make-up-air requirements and local code with the relevant professionals. Then set the spring line, which is where the vertical sides begin to curve. Keep adjacent cabinetry simple and leave enough plain wall around the arch for its shape to remain legible.

Why an arched range hood changes the entire kitchen

Kitchens contain a great deal of visual measurement: cabinet grids, tile joints, appliance rectangles, drawer lines and worktop edges. A single curve interrupts that discipline without making the room less functional. The eye reads it as shelter, almost like a small hearth, which gives the cooking wall more depth than a flat metal hood.

The effect is strongest when the room is otherwise fairly straight. Add an arched pantry door, curved island, scalloped backsplash and round pendant to the same view and the gesture loses authority. One confident curve looks architectural. Five related curves look like a theme selected from a catalogue.

The arch should have a reason to begin and end

The best examples grow from full-height side returns, cabinet edges or a chimney breast. Their spring line aligns with something nearby, such as the top of wall cabinets or a stone shelf. An arch floating over the range with no relationship to the room can resemble a decorative cut-out.

Before changing the wall, photograph it straight on and trace several options over the image. A shallow segmental arch feels calm and broad. A semicircle is more pronounced. A narrow tall arch introduces Mediterranean or historic associations. Choose the geometry that belongs to the ceiling height and cabinet rhythm, not the one receiving the most saves that week.

Ventilation comes before plaster

The Home Ventilating Institute describes a range hood as an enclosure and fan designed to capture heat, odours, gases, grease, steam and smoke, then exhaust those contaminants outdoors. The decorative shell does not perform that work. The insert, fan and duct system do.

Start with the cooking appliance and the manufacturer’s requirements. Gas, electric and induction equipment can have different clearance and ventilation needs. High-output cooking, wide ranges and frequent frying may require greater capture area or airflow. Local building rules may also trigger make-up air, fire-safety or ducting requirements. This is the part to confirm with a qualified kitchen designer, ventilation professional and relevant trades before framing begins.

Useful HVI reference figures

HVI guidance recommends that a wall-mounted hood match the width of the cooking range and be installed at the manufacturer’s recommended height. Its consumer guide gives the following reference airflow figures for wall locations. They are guidance, not a substitute for appliance instructions or local code.

36 inches300 CFM120 CFM
48 inches400 CFM160 CFM

Airflow alone does not guarantee performance. HVI notes that duct width, bends and length affect efficiency, while sound depends on the hood, fan, airflow and ducting. A short, correctly sized duct route with fewer turns usually gives the insert a better chance of delivering its rated performance. Decide where the duct exits before settling the curve or adding a decorative shelf.

Leave the machinery serviceable

Filters need to come out. Lights and controls need to remain reachable. The fan may eventually require repair. Ask the installer to demonstrate every access point before plasterboard, wood panelling or stone closes the enclosure. Photograph the framing and duct route during construction and keep the model details with the house records.

How to get the arch proportion right

Begin with three horizontal measurements: range width, hood width and total wall width. The hood should not appear narrower than the appliance beneath it. On a large wall, a hood that exactly matches the range can still look pinched if tall cabinets press against both sides. Small breathing gaps or shallow open shelves may help, provided they remain outside heat and clearance requirements.

Set the vertical sides before drawing the curve

A common mistake is beginning the arch too low. This creates a cartoonish semicircle and visually squeezes the cooking area. Let the sides rise vertically from the worktop zone, then begin the curve at a height related to nearby cabinetry. A higher spring line produces a quieter, more architectural silhouette.

The crown, which is the highest point of the arch, needs room beneath the ceiling. If it nearly touches coving, a beam or a pendant line, the hood can feel swollen. Lowering the crown or choosing a shallower arc often looks more expensive than forcing a perfect half-circle into insufficient space.

Thickness should look believable

A paper-thin arch reads as applied decoration. An excessively deep arch consumes worktop light and can make the cook feel enclosed. Show enough side return to suggest structure while keeping the working area open. The insert depth and capture needs set a practical minimum, so model the view from standing height before approving the final profile.

Use taped outlines on the wall or a full-size cardboard template. Small drawings are forgiving. A life-size curve exposes awkward shoulders, a crown that is too high and side returns that steal more space than expected.

Plaster, wood, tile or stone

The finish decides whether the arch feels quiet or ornamental. It also determines cleaning, edge detail, weight and construction sequence.

FinishStrengthWatch for
Smooth plasterSeamless, soft and easy to blend with wallsCracking, unsuitable coatings and hard-to-clean ledges
Limewash effectClouded depth and old-wall characterFinish compatibility near heat and grease
WoodWarmth and connection to cabinetryMovement, edge joints and required clearances
TileDurable colour and visible craftComplex cuts around the curve and grout maintenance
StoneStrong monolithic presenceWeight, support, cost and visual heaviness

Smooth plaster gives the clearest silhouette. Match the wall colour if you want the hood to feel built in. Choose a subtly different mineral texture if the kitchen needs depth. Confirm that every layer of the assembly is appropriate for the location and can be maintained near grease and heat.

Wood works best when it repeats cabinetry without copying every door detail. Vertical grain can make a low ceiling feel taller. Horizontal boards emphasise width. Keep mouldings restrained so the arch remains the main event.

Tile can follow the inner reveal or cover the whole face, but the curve creates demanding cuts. Mock up the edge condition before ordering. A skilled installer can turn those cuts into craft; a rushed border will advertise every compromise.

Stone needs enough room and structural planning. A shallow stone frame around plaster may deliver the material without the mass of a fully clad hood. This approach connects naturally to the controlled stone gestures in our red marble bathroom guide.

An arched hood can work in a small kitchen

Scale down the depth and ornament, not the clarity. In a compact galley, a shallow curve contained within one cabinet bay can make the cooking area feel intentional without consuming the entire wall. Keep the finish close to the wall colour, use a modest range width and avoid thick side shelves.

A dark tile backsplash can sharpen a pale arch by making the opening legible. In a narrow room, carry the backsplash only where it can be cleaned easily and let surrounding surfaces stay quiet. Continuous colour prevents the small hood from breaking into too many components.

In a large kitchen, resist making it enormous

More wall does not require a more inflated arch. A 48-inch range already provides scale. The hood can match it and rely on proportion rather than decorative bulk. Tall side cabinets or a simple ledge can complete the elevation without turning the cooking wall into a stage set.

Coordinate the hood with the colours already carrying the room. Our guide to kitchen colour combinations explains why timber, stone and painted cabinetry should form one sequence rather than three isolated choices. The arch is not an excuse to restart the palette.

Lighting belongs outside the curve as well as inside it

Integrated task lights should illuminate the cooking surface according to the hood maker’s design. Ambient light from a nearby window or ceiling fixture should reveal the plaster shape without casting a harsh shadow across the range. A picture light placed above the crown usually complicates the silhouette and introduces heat or wiring decisions for little gain.

Eight arched range hood mistakes to avoid

  1. Drawing the curve before choosing the insert. The machinery sets width, depth, access and clearance.
  2. Making the arch narrower than the range. It looks pinched and may reduce the intended capture area.
  3. Starting the curve too low. A higher spring line usually feels calmer.
  4. Ignoring the duct route. Long runs and multiple bends can affect performance and noise.
  5. Closing access panels permanently. Filters, lights and fans need maintenance.
  6. Adding every curved trend nearby. One arch is architecture; repetition becomes theme.
  7. Choosing finish by photograph alone. Ask how it cleans, repairs and behaves near heat.
  8. Forgetting the standing view. Check the hood from the sink, island, doorway and actual cooking position.

If the room already has an awkward boxed hood, a skilled designer may be able to simplify its face without replacing the whole ventilation system. Do not assume the old enclosure is empty. Locate ductwork, wiring, framing and fire-rated assemblies before opening anything.

The same principle runs through our guide to making a home look expensive: proportion and installation quality create more value than extra ornament. An honest plaster curve with a quiet edge will outlast a more expensive assembly that never resolved its joins.

Arched range hood questions

Are arched range hoods just a trend?

Search interest has risen sharply in 2026, but arched hearth and chimney forms have much older architectural precedent. A simple, well-proportioned version can outlast the current trend cycle.

How wide should an arched range hood be?

Follow the appliance and hood manufacturer requirements. HVI guidance says a wall hood should match the width of the cooking range. Some installations may benefit from additional capture width.

Can an arched hood work with low ceilings?

Yes. Use a shallow segmental curve, begin it higher on straight sides and leave visible space between the crown and ceiling.

What is the easiest arched hood finish to live with?

A smooth, professionally specified finish with few ledges is usually simplest visually. Cleaning and durability depend on the complete system, so confirm the coating and substrate with the installer.

The curve is finished when the kitchen stops performing

A good arch does not need a second arch to explain it. It holds the range, hides the necessary machinery and gives a hard-working wall one moment of softness. Once the proportion is right, let the cooktop, timber and daylight do the rest.
